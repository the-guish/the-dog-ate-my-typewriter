# Individual Contributors and Juniors: How Generative/Agentic AI Changed Workload, Information Volume, and Entry-Level Prospects (2026 publications, Jan–Oct 2026)

Scope note for the report writer: This file covers the bottom of the management pyramid — entry-level hires, juniors (0–3 years), and senior individual contributors without direct reports. Date window is Jan–Oct 2026. Items dated 2025 are explicitly marked "2025 context" and used only where a 2026 source leans on them. Items whose 2026 date or primary text could not be confirmed are flagged inline and repeated in Gaps. Primary sources were fetched directly for: Stanford Canaries (Aug 2026), Dallas Fed (Jan 2026, via search summary of the Fed page), NY Fed Liberty Street (May 2026), Census Bureau CES-WP-26-56 (Sept 2026), Indeed Hiring Lab (July 2026), Microsoft Work Trend Index 2026 (May 2026), Gallup Q1 2026 Workforce Study (Apr 2026), Census HTOPS story (Aug 2026), Journal of Accountancy coverage of Korn Ferry Workforce 2026 (Sept 2026) and ResumeTemplates (Feb 2026), MIT Technology Review (May 2026). Figures from secondary coverage are labeled as such.

Research-approach note: Roughly 24 search/fetch calls were made. Evidence is strongest on entry-level hiring (observed payroll/admin/postings data from multiple institutions) and weakest on manager-to-IC message volume (no 2026 data found). Workload evidence is almost entirely self-reported survey data.

## Key Question 1: What do 2026 studies say about entry-level hiring and junior headcount in AI-exposed occupations?

### Takeaway
Every observed-data study published in 2026 (ADP payroll via Stanford and Dallas Fed; Census administrative records; Indeed and Revelio postings) finds that the AI-era labor adjustment has fallen on the youngest workers in AI-exposed occupations, operating through reduced hiring rather than layoffs, with the Stanford gap widening from ~15% to ~19% between mid-2025 and June 2026. The notable dissent is the New York Fed (May 2026), which, using Lightcast postings, finds junior and senior postings in exposed occupations moving in parallel and concludes AI is "not the main driver" of the entry-level slowdown.

### Cited Findings

**Stanford Digital Economy Lab — "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" revised version (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen), published August 12, 2026 (original Aug 2025 = 2025 context):**
- Workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations are ~19% below the employment level they would have had if they had kept pace with less-exposed occupations, as of June 2026; the gap was ~15% at the July 2025 data vintage. "Experienced workers show no comparable gap." — [Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 12, 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)
- In levels: employment of 22–25-year-olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell ~11% from November 2022 to June 2026, while the same age group in the three least-exposed quintiles grew ~10%. — [Stanford, Aug 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)
- Fact 4: adjustment operates "primarily through reduced hiring of young workers, not increased separations." Fact 5: declines concentrated where AI is used to automate tasks; where AI complements workers, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced workers. Fact 6: adjustment shows up in employment, not base pay. — [Stanford, Aug 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)
- Data: ADP payroll data; exposure measure from Eloundou et al. (2024); data range Nov 2022–June 2026. Findings are explicitly "descriptive, not causal." Authors caution that gaps shrink when education is accounted for, that some differential trends predate widespread generative AI use, and that estimated gaps are larger in the ADP sample than in national survey benchmarks (discrepancy concentrated in education, health care, public administration). — [Stanford, Aug 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/); full PDF: [Canaries_August2026.pdf](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)
- Authors update results monthly on a public dashboard. — [Canaries Dashboard](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/)
- Secondary coverage disagrees on the prior-vintage baseline (15% vs 13%); the primary Stanford page says 15% (July 2025 vintage). — [AI Weekly (secondary)](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/stanfords-brynjolfsson-study-entry-level-employment-in-ai-exposed-jobs-now-19); [Stanford primary](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)

**Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (Tyler Atkinson and Shane Yamco), published January 6, 2026:**
- Young workers' employment dropped in high-AI-exposure occupations; the decline "is primarily driven by a decline in people transitioning directly from out of the workforce into employment rather than by layoffs or low job-finding rates among the unemployed." Implies "only a slight impact on the aggregate unemployment rate so far." — [Dallas Fed, Jan 6, 2026](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0106)
- Data: ADP payroll data on ~25 million workers; exposure measure from Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, Rock; cross-checked against CPS microdata. — [Dallas Fed, Jan 6, 2026](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0106)
- The 13% employment decline for 22–25-year-olds cited in coverage of the Dallas Fed piece is the earlier Stanford figure that the Fed authors cite, not an independent Dallas Fed estimate. — [Houston Chronicle (secondary)](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/business/article/ai-unemployment-jobs-texas-21282196.php)
- Secondary sources report an employment-share drop for ages 20–24 in AI-exposed occupations from ~16.4% to ~15.5% (Nov 2022–Sept 2025); this could not be confirmed against the Fed's own text. — [Intuition Labs (secondary, unverified)](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/ai-entry-level-employment-hiring-data)

**U.S. Census Bureau Center for Economic Studies working paper CES-WP-26-56, "Graduating into Disruption: Labor Market Outcomes for AI-Exposed College Majors" (Cody Orr, Lee C. Tucker, Lawrence Warren), September 2026:**
- After ChatGPT's late-2022 release, graduates in the most AI-exposed decile of college majors saw their likelihood of initial employment fall by 5 percentage points and full-quarter initial earnings fall by ~13% relative to less-exposed majors. The authors call the earnings decline "comparable in magnitude to the earnings losses associated with graduating into a large recession." — [Census Bureau CES-WP-26-56](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html); [PDF](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-56.pdf)
- Mechanism: roughly half of the earnings decline is lower pay within hiring industries, the rest a shift toward lower-wage sectors. The gap narrows to ~5% after two years but remains substantial for the most exposed majors (largely computer science and related). — [Chronicle of Higher Education (secondary)](https://www.chronicle.com/article/the-most-ai-exposed-college-graduates-are-entering-another-great-recession)
- Data: administrative records covering institutions awarding ~29% of U.S. bachelor's degrees, 2016–2024, ~6.7 million graduate records. Design assumes parallel trends; pandemic and remote-work shocks not fully controlled. — [Census Bureau CES-WP-26-56](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html)

**Independent replication in Census data (Tucker, 2026), reported via secondary economics commentary:**
- Mapping occupational AI exposure onto industries using Census data covering nearly all private-sector employment, hires of 22–24-year-olds in the most exposed fifth of industries dropped ~9% right after ChatGPT's release and have not recovered. — [Alex Imas, Substack (secondary; primary Tucker 2026 paper not fetched)](https://aleximas.substack.com/p/has-ai-impacted-the-labor-market)

**Federal Reserve Bank of New York — Liberty Street Economics (Richard Audoly, Miles Guerin, Giorgio Topa), "Do Job Postings Show Early Labor-Market Effects of AI?", May 14, 2026 — the main dissent:**
- Using Lightcast postings and Anthropic's task-level exposure metric (0–1 scale, aggregated to occupations), an event study relative to 2022:Q3 finds junior and senior postings within highly exposed occupations "moving broadly in parallel" after 2022; "the slowdown in postings is not concentrated specifically in entry-level highly exposed jobs." — [NY Fed Liberty Street, May 14, 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/)
- "The divergence between high- and low-exposure occupations began before 2022"; no additional break after 2022; gap stabilizes after 2023. Conclusion: AI "is not the main driver of the slowdown in hiring"; firms surveyed by the NY Fed plan to address AI mainly via retraining with limited hiring effects. — [NY Fed, May 14, 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/)
- Context numbers: fewer than 10% of workers and vacancies are in occupations with exposure ≥0.4; ~40% of workers are in jobs with zero measured exposure. — [NY Fed, May 14, 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/)
- A follow-up NY Fed post (Aug 2026) frames AI's labor impact so far as mainly changing skill requirements rather than eliminating jobs. — [NY Fed Liberty Street, Aug 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/08/ais-impact-on-labor-and-hiring/)
- Federal Reserve Board FEDS Note (March 27, 2026): "no evidence of a reduction in job postings for industries or firms which have higher levels of AI adoption," while acknowledging "pockets of workers who are experiencing a disproportionately difficult job search." — [Federal Reserve Board FEDS Notes, Mar 27, 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/ai-adoption-and-firms-job-posting-behavior-20260327.html)

**Indeed Hiring Lab (Felix Aidala and Sneha Puri), "The Labor Market Is Tilting Toward Seniority," July 23, 2026:**
- Senior postings up 14.7% year-over-year as of May 2026; entry-level postings down 7.5% YoY as of May 2026 and declining since a 2022 peak. Versus January 2025: senior +13.5%, mid-level −6.7%, entry-level −6.3% (body text says −6.2%; the two figures conflict within the article). — [Indeed Hiring Lab, July 23, 2026](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/the-labor-market-is-tilting-toward-seniority/)
- Software development Q1 2026: senior roles 69.3% of postings, entry-level 4.5% (lowest entry share of any tracked sector). Tech's senior share rose 9 points 2019–2025; mid-level fell 7.7 points; entry-level fell just over 1 point. — [Indeed Hiring Lab, July 23, 2026](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/the-labor-market-is-tilting-toward-seniority/)
- Sectors with high AI exposure or remote-work potential tend to have fewer entry-level roles. Indeed explicitly declines to attribute causation to AI, listing remote work, Fed rate hikes, and post-pandemic hiring corrections as alternatives that "may be at least partially at play." Method: algorithmic classification of responsibilities/training requirements (Entry ≈0–1 yrs, Mid 2–4, Senior 5+), ~95% accuracy on manual check. — [Indeed Hiring Lab, July 23, 2026](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/the-labor-market-is-tilting-toward-seniority/)
- Indeed Hiring Lab, Sept 17, 2026: the advertised-pay premium in AI-exposed jobs "appears to widen with seniority – large for senior roles, moderate at mid-level, and negligible at entry level" (~2-point gap at entry level; regression terms mostly not statistically significant). — [Indeed Hiring Lab, Sept 17, 2026](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/09/17/ai-exposure-isnt-squeezing-advertised-pay-in-the-us-its-boosting-it/)
- Secondary coverage of the Sept 2026 Indeed analysis reports that among salaried postings in the most AI-exposed occupations, the entry-level share fell from 29% (2021) to 10% (2026) while the senior share rose from 22% to 47%; Indeed notes salaried postings skew senior (~37% senior vs ~14% across all US postings). The 29%→10% figure was not visible in Indeed's own page text and should be treated as secondary. — [AI Signal (secondary)](https://aisignal.day/en/ai-exposed-pay-2026/)
- Indeed Hiring Lab, July 8, 2026: 71% of the increase in US software development postings between May 2025 and May 2026 came from senior roles (figure from search summary of the Indeed post). — [Indeed Hiring Lab, July 8, 2026](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-is-no-longer-just-a-tech-occupation-story/)

**Revelio Labs (2026):**
- U.S. entry-level postings fell ~35% since early 2023 (as cited by Fast Company); Revelio itself says the drop alone does not prove AI causation. — [Revelio Labs blog](https://www.reveliolabs.com/blog/fast-company-entry-level-hiring)

- October 22, 2026 (Bloomberg, citing Revelio): entry-level roles at US banks fell almost 5% since 2022 while mid- and senior-level employment kept growing. — [Bloomberg, Oct 22, 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/banks-have-fewer-entry-level-jobs-but-higher-ranks-are-growing)

**Graduate labor-market indicators cited in MIT Technology Review (Georgios Petropoulos, USC Marshall), May 26, 2026:**

### Inferences
- The disagreement between Stanford/Dallas Fed/Census (employment and hires data) and the NY Fed (postings data) is partly a measurement artifact: postings measure advertised demand, while payroll/administrative data measure realized hires. If firms post junior roles but fill fewer of them, or fill them with more experienced candidates, postings would move in parallel while realized junior employment falls. The Indeed finding that entry-level postings themselves are declining (−7.5% YoY) sits between the two camps.
- The Ramp–Revelio firm-level finding (heavy AI adopters grew headcount ~10%) and the occupation-level findings are reconcilable: AI-heavy firms may be growing overall while shifting their hiring mix toward senior roles, which is exactly what Indeed's seniority tilt shows.
- Because the Stanford adjustment is in employment rather than base pay, and the Census paper finds a 13% initial-earnings hit via industry/sector shifts, the cost to juniors appears as fewer or worse first jobs, not as lower pay in the same jobs.

### Gaps
- No 2026 ADP Research Institute standalone publication on entry-level hiring was located (ADP data reaches this topic only via Stanford and Dallas Fed).
- No 2026 LinkedIn Economic Graph or Burning Glass Institute primary report with entry-level headcount figures was fetched; BGI details come from a summary page only.
- PwC 2026 AI Jobs Barometer was cited in secondary coverage as finding that AI-exposed entry-level roles "increasingly require senior-level skills," but the primary report was not located and the claim could not be verified.
- The Anthropic March 2026 report cited by MIT Technology Review as "suggestive evidence" was not fetched; its contents are unknown.
- No 2026 data was found on junior headcount outside the U.S. (OECD Employment Outlook 2026, ILO, European data) within the call budget.
- The Tucker (2026) Census replication is known only through a secondary Substack summary.

## Key Question 2: Are individual contributors reporting higher or lower workload since adopting AI? Any 2026 data on hours, task volume, or "AI-driven intensification"?

### Takeaway
Every 2026 worker survey located reports net workload increases alongside AI adoption — Korn Ferry (16,000+): 52% say AI increased the number of tasks expected of them; SHRM (5,875): more than 3 in 5 report higher work volume and 54% a faster pace; ResumeTemplates (1,250): 31% workload up vs 16% down. All of this is self-reported; no 2026 study was found that measures actual hours worked by ICs before and after AI adoption.

### Cited Findings

**Korn Ferry, Workforce 2026 Global Insights Report (16,000+ employees), as reported by Bryan Strickland, Journal of Accountancy (AICPA & CIMA), September 15, 2026:**
- 63% say AI has improved their efficiency; 52% say AI tools have increased the number of tasks expected of them; 62% say their workload has grown significantly over the past two years; 61% perform the responsibilities of more than one role; 49% are exhausted by the pace of change; 45% describe themselves as "too busy to deliver meaningful results"; 64% say their company prioritizes efficiency gains over finding new ways to add value; 58% say they are fairly rewarded for taking on additional roles; 39% say a lack of managers has left them feeling directionless. — [Journal of Accountancy, Sept 15, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/sep/driving-efficiency-or-driving-workers-toward-burnout-how-ai-is-being-used/)
- Korn Ferry's Jenna Young: "Can we sustain people feeling like they're permanently working two jobs? ... Not for long." The article does not break results out by job level. — [Journal of Accountancy, Sept 15, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/sep/driving-efficiency-or-driving-workers-toward-burnout-how-ai-is-being-used/)

**SHRM, "Navigating AI in the Workplace: 2026" (5,875 U.S.-based workers surveyed March–April 2026) — the only 2026 source found that splits workload/expectation results by job level:**
- Half of workers say AI tools have raised performance expectations; 70% of directors and above report increased expectations vs 55% of managers vs 35% of individual contributors. — [SHRM full report](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)
- More than 3 in 5 workers report an increase in overall volume of work; 65% say the value of their work has increased; 54% say the pace of work has increased since AI tools were introduced. — [SHRM full report](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)
- Nearly 3 in 5 director-and-above respondents report frequent references to AI in performance conversations (no IC figure given in the summary). — [SHRM full report](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)
- Note: these figures come from a search summary quoting the SHRM report page; the full methodology and question wording were not fetched.

**ResumeTemplates.com survey (1,250 U.S. corporate workers who spend much of their time on a computer; no field dates or margin of error reported), as reported by Bryan Strickland, Journal of Accountancy, February 20, 2026:**
- 31% say workload increased after AI was introduced at their company; 16% say it decreased. Of those reporting increases, 43% say workload at least doubled. 59% say AI increased pressure to perform at a higher level; 37% report "AI fatigue"; 46% feel confident they could keep up without AI; 40% worry AI could replace them within five years. — [Journal of Accountancy, Feb 20, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/feb/can-ai-actually-lead-to-a-heavier-workload/)
- Julia Toothacre (ResumeTemplates chief career strategist) attributes rising workloads to layoffs/reorganizations leaving fewer people, and warns of "workload creep," where managers assume AI lets employees take on more without added resources. — [Journal of Accountancy, Feb 20, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/feb/can-ai-actually-lead-to-a-heavier-workload/)

**UC Berkeley field study published in Harvard Business Review (~200 employees at a U.S. tech company tracked for eight months), February 2026 — FLAGGED: primary HBR article not fetched; details from two secondary write-ups:**
- Secondary coverage states the study found AI tools did not reduce work but "consistently intensified it," and that participants felt more productive but not less busy; one secondary blog reports 83% said AI increased their workload. — [UC Today (secondary)](https://www.uctoday.com/productivity-automation/more-ai-more-work-what-the-infinite-workday-means-for-uc-leaders/); [Lovex (secondary; 83% figure unverified)](https://lovex.dev/blog/ai-work-intensification)

**Microsoft 2026 Work Trend Index Annual Report, "Agents, Human Agency, and the Opportunity for Every Organization," May 5, 2026 (survey fielded by Edelman DxI Feb 18–Apr 7/20, 2026; n=20,000 full-time knowledge workers who use AI, 2,000 per market across 10 markets; plus 105,000 Copilot chat samples from one week in Feb 2026, North America commercial):**

### Inferences
- The SHRM level split (70% directors / 55% managers / 35% ICs reporting raised expectations) suggests the expectation shock is currently felt most acutely higher up the pyramid, while the volume/pace increases (>3 in 5; 54%) are reported across the workforce. Combined with Korn Ferry's "39% directionless due to lack of managers" and "61% performing more than one role," the IC-level experience looks like more tasks with less supervision rather than explicitly higher targets.
- Microsoft's 13%-rewarded vs 65%-fear-falling-behind gap, and the 2:1 leader-vs-employee gap on "reinvention is rewarded," imply ICs are absorbing adoption pressure without a corresponding recognition structure.
- The Census time-saved distribution (56% of weekly AI users save ≤2 hours, 14% save none or lose time) suggests reported "productivity gains" at the IC level are modest and uneven, which makes any expectation of large output increases per person a likely source of intensification.

### Gaps
- No 2026 source measuring actual hours worked (timesheets, telemetry) by ICs before vs after AI adoption was found; all workload data is self-reported.
- Korn Ferry's primary Workforce 2026 report was not fetched; no job-level breakdown is known.
- The UC Berkeley/HBR 8-month intensification study is known only through secondary coverage; its exact title, authors, and the 83% figure are unverified.

## Key Question 3: How much AI-generated content are ICs asked to produce and consume? Is "workslop" hitting ICs harder than managers?

### Takeaway
2026 sources extend the "workslop" concept from individual annoyance to organizational knowledge decay (HBR, Holweg and Davenport, June 2026) and report that 44% of AI-using workers describe their own output as "AI slop" (SHRM 2026), while Microsoft finds 50% of AI users rank quality control of AI output as the most important human skill. No 2026 source was found that measures whether ICs receive more workslop than managers; the 2025 BetterUp/Stanford baseline (41% received workslop in a month; ~2 hours rework per incident) has not been re-fielded in any 2026 publication located.

### Cited Findings

**2025 context (foundational, referenced by 2026 sources):** BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab, HBR, September 2025; online survey of 1,150 U.S. full-time desk workers; ~40–41% received workslop in the prior month; ~2 hours of rework per incident; ~$186/month per worker; ~$9 million/year for a 10,000-person organization; 53% annoyed, 38% confused, 22% offended. — [The Register, Sept 26, 2025 (2025 context)](https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/26/ai_workslop_productivity/); [Entrepreneur (2025 context)](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/ai-workslop-is-a-9-million-issue-stanford-betterup-study/497483)

**Harvard Business Review, "Don't Let AI Slop Muck Up Your Company's Processes" (Matthias Holweg, Oxford Saïd; Thomas H. Davenport, Babson/MIT IDE), June 16, 2026:**
- Frames decay in the accuracy and quality of organizational knowledge as the organization-level version of "workslop": when low-quality AI output moves through processes in sequence, errors accumulate and productivity gains disappear; leaders' challenges framed as verification, validation, and entropy. Only the summary/introduction was retrievable; body data points and recommendations not extracted; no new survey data confirmed. — [HBR, June 16, 2026](https://hbr.org/2026/06/dont-let-ai-slop-muck-up-your-companys-processes); [TNW coverage](https://thenextweb.com/news/ai-workslop-knowledge-decay-harvard-business-review-productivity)

- Among the 41% of workers who use AI, 44% describe their own output as "AI slop" (from the report's worker summary as quoted in search results). — [SHRM full report](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)

**Microsoft 2026 Work Trend Index, May 5, 2026 (n=20,000 AI users):**
- 50% rank quality control of AI output as the most important human skill; 46% rank critical thinking; 86% treat AI output as a starting point rather than a final answer. Frontier Professionals are more likely to deliberately decide which tasks go to AI vs humans (53% vs 33%) and to do some work without AI (43% vs 30%). — [Microsoft WorkLab, May 5, 2026](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization)
- Telemetry: 49% of Microsoft 365 Copilot conversations support cognitive work (analysis, decision-making, evaluation, creative thinking); 19% working with people; 17% producing outputs; 15% finding information. Active agents across Microsoft 365 grew 15x year over year (18x in large enterprises). — [Microsoft WorkLab, May 5, 2026](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization)

**Census HTOPS March 2026 (published Aug 2026):** the most common IC-level AI tasks are information search (37%), writing communications/documentation (32%), idea generation (32%), summarizing (31%) — i.e., the task mix is dominated by producing and consuming text. — [Census Bureau, Aug 11, 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/08/ai-use-at-work.html)

**Information-volume baseline (2025 context, no 2026 update found):** Microsoft's "Breaking Down the Infinite Workday" (June 2025): average worker receives 153 Teams messages per weekday; interrupted every two minutes; 50+ messages outside core hours; message volume up 6% YoY. — [Microsoft WorkLab, June 2025 (2025 context)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/breaking-down-infinite-workday); 2026 commentary referencing it: [UC Today](https://www.uctoday.com/productivity-automation/more-ai-more-work-what-the-infinite-workday-means-for-uc-leaders/)

**Pre-2026 context on review burden (date not confirmed as 2026):** Upwork reported employees citing more time reviewing or moderating AI-generated content (39%), more time learning tools (23%), and being asked to do more work as a direct result of AI (21%). — [Upwork press release (likely 2024; flagged)](https://www.upwork.com/press/releases/upwork-study-finds-employee-workloads-rising-despite-increased-c-suite-investment-in-artificial-intelligence)

### Inferences
- Taken together, SHRM's 44% self-described slop rate and Microsoft's finding that half of AI users rank output quality control as the top skill suggest that reviewing AI-generated text has become a core, and largely unmeasured, part of IC work in 2026.
- Holweg and Davenport's process-level framing implies that whoever sits downstream in a workflow absorbs accumulated verification burden; ICs and juniors are disproportionately downstream (they execute, draft, and check), so the structural logic points to ICs bearing more of the workslop cost than managers — but this is inference, not a 2026 measurement.

### Gaps

- No 2026 Microsoft telemetry update on message/email/meeting volume per worker was found (the 2026 Work Trend Index does not cover it).
- The body of the June 2026 HBR Holweg/Davenport article could not be retrieved; whether it contains new quantitative data is unknown.
- No 2026 data on the volume of AI-generated documentation or agent outputs that ICs are asked to review (e.g., pull requests from coding agents) was located.

## Key Question 4: Are juniors being asked to supervise/verify AI output instead of doing the work, and how does this change their learning and load?

### Takeaway
2026 academic and practitioner sources converge on the claim that AI is absorbing the drafting, triage, coding, and summarizing tasks through which juniors historically learned, and that juniors are now expected to verify output they lack the experience to judge; the HBR/KPMG–UT Austin field study of 523 early-career staff (July 2026) finds that foundational skills do not predict which juniors work well with AI. Direct evidence that verification is hurting learning comes from a small Anthropic experiment (n=52; AI-assisted group scored 50% vs 67%) whose primary text and exact date (Jan vs Feb 2026) were not confirmed.

### Cited Findings

**Harvard Business Review, "Research: Why Some Junior Employees Work Well with AI—and Others Don't" (Ashish Agarwal, Anitesh Barua, Fangchen Song, Wen Wen — UT Austin McCombs; Anu Puvvada — KPMG Studio), July 23, 2026:**
- Framing from the article's introduction: entry-level work traditionally included tasks that built industry expertise; many are now "at risk of being delegated to AI-powered workflows"; AI is "resetting the baseline," meaning the standard for acceptable output rises as models improve; organizations need to understand how individuals add value beyond the AI baseline in higher-stakes work requiring judgment. — [HBR, July 23, 2026](https://hbr.org/2026/07/research-why-some-junior-employees-work-well-with-ai-and-others-dont)
- Per search summary (body not retrievable): a field study of 523 early-career KPMG staff sorted workers into three groups — AI apprentices, AI delegators, and AI amplifiers — and found that foundational skills such as critical thinking and domain knowledge did not reliably predict group membership. Percentages for each archetype were not retrievable. — [HBR, July 23, 2026 (details flagged as from search summary)](https://hbr.org/2026/07/research-why-some-junior-employees-work-well-with-ai-and-others-dont)

**Anthropic learning experiment (52 mostly junior engineers learning a Python library), early 2026 — FLAGGED: primary not fetched; date reported as January or February 2026 by different sources:**
- Average knowledge-test scores were 50% with AI assistance vs 67% without (~17-point gap); the group using AI for conceptual questions did better than the group using it to write code; completion-time difference not statistically significant; test measured immediate recall, not long-term competence. — [CHRO Association commentary (secondary)](https://www.chro.org/w/who-will-train-the-next-generation-if-ai-takes-the-first-draft-); [Allwork.space, May 2026 (secondary)](https://allwork.space/2026/05/ai-apprenticeship-is-the-new-career-ladder-for-a-workforce-losing-its-training-ground/)

**MIT Technology Review, Georgios Petropoulos (USC Marshall), May 26, 2026:**
- Entry-level work "functions as part of the economy's training system": junior analysts learn which data can be trusted, junior developers learn how production systems fail, early-career legal/financial staff learn how rules, judgment, deadlines, and relationships interact. If AI absorbs drafting, triage, coding, summarizing, and administrative prep, "firms may gain short-run efficiency while society loses long-run capability." — [MIT Technology Review, May 26, 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/26/1137865/its-time-to-address-the-looming-crisis-in-entry-level-work/)
- What juniors are now expected to do: supervise and verify AI output, understand its limits, combine it with human expertise; build AI literacy, data literacy, prompt-based workflow skills, verification skills, and domain judgment. The author argues the "learn to code" premise no longer holds because AI handles the routine coding layer. Policy asks: wage subsidies/tax credits for early-career hiring into AI-augmented roles; paid co-ops and apprenticeships. — [MIT Technology Review, May 26, 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/26/1137865/its-time-to-address-the-looming-crisis-in-entry-level-work/)

**Stanford Canaries (Aug 2026) mechanism:** the paper distinguishes codified knowledge (declining employment for young workers) from tacit knowledge (rising employment for experienced workers) as a possible mechanism — i.e., AI substitutes for the book-learned skills juniors bring, and complements the experience-based judgment seniors bring. — [Stanford, Aug 12, 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)

**Practitioner guidance (2026, non-peer-reviewed):**
- CHRO Association (citing McKinsey) recommends an "attempt-then-check" loop in which juniors form their own view before comparing to AI output, and that managers coach judgment rather than task completion. — [CHRO Association](https://www.chro.org/w/who-will-train-the-next-generation-if-ai-takes-the-first-draft-)
- A consultancy piece argues reviewing AI output depends on skills that producing work used to build; newcomers either accept output uncritically (automation bias) or check everything at the same depth. — [Arquin.ai (practitioner blog)](https://arquin.ai/insight/how-will-junior-staff-learn-if-ai-does-the-entry-level-work/)
- Developer commentary, May 2026: "AI Ate the Apprenticeship." — [dev-craft.be](https://www.dev-craft.be/2026/05/ai-ate-the-apprenticeship/)
- Allwork.space, May 2026: "AI Apprenticeship Is The New Career Ladder For A Workforce Losing Its Training Ground." — [Allwork.space, May 2026](https://allwork.space/2026/05/ai-apprenticeship-is-the-new-career-ladder-for-a-workforce-losing-its-training-ground/)

**Microsoft WTI 2026 skill expectations (all AI users, not junior-specific):** 50% rank quality control of AI output as the most important human skill; 46% critical thinking; 86% treat AI output as a starting point. — [Microsoft WorkLab, May 5, 2026](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization)

### Inferences
- The combination of (a) Stanford's codified-vs-tacit mechanism, (b) the HBR/KPMG "resetting the baseline" framing, and (c) the Anthropic recall result implies a trap for juniors: the tasks AI removes are the ones that built the tacit judgment needed to verify AI, so verification duties are being pushed onto the people least equipped to perform them, and the practice that would equip them is disappearing.
- The HBR/KPMG finding that foundational skills don't predict who thrives with AI suggests the differentiator is working style (how one chooses to use AI) rather than prior competence — which argues against screening juniors harder and for coaching usage patterns.

### Gaps
- The full text of the HBR/KPMG study (archetype percentages, productivity/quality differences by archetype, recommendations) could not be retrieved.
- The Anthropic 52-person study's primary publication, exact date, and title were not confirmed.
- No 2026 study measuring juniors' time allocation (share of day spent reviewing AI output vs producing original work) was found.
- No 2026 longitudinal evidence on whether AI-era juniors' skill growth lags earlier cohorts was located; all learning claims rest on one small experiment plus argument.

## Key Question 6: Are productivity gains translating into reduced hours for ICs, or into more output expected per person?

### Takeaway
2026 surveys consistently show time savings being absorbed as higher task expectations rather than shorter hours: Korn Ferry finds 63% report efficiency gains but 52% report more tasks expected and 64% say their company prioritizes efficiency over new value; SHRM finds >3 in 5 report higher volume and 54% faster pace; Microsoft finds only 13% are rewarded for AI-driven reinvention. No 2026 source reported any population of ICs working fewer hours because of AI.

### Cited Findings
- Korn Ferry Workforce 2026 (16,000+): 63% say AI improved efficiency; 52% say AI increased tasks expected of them; 62% say workload grew significantly over two years; 64% say company prioritizes efficiency gains over finding new ways to add value; 58% feel fairly rewarded for additional roles. — [Journal of Accountancy, Sept 15, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/sep/driving-efficiency-or-driving-workers-toward-burnout-how-ai-is-being-used/)
- SHRM 2026 (5,875): >3 in 5 report higher work volume; 65% say value of work increased; 54% say pace increased; 50% say expectations raised (70% directors+, 55% managers, 35% ICs). — [SHRM full report](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)
- ResumeTemplates (1,250): 31% workload up vs 16% down; 59% more pressure to perform at a higher level; Toothacre's "workload creep" — managers assume AI lets employees take on more without added resources. — [Journal of Accountancy, Feb 20, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/feb/can-ai-actually-lead-to-a-heavier-workload/)
- Journal of Accountancy (Feb 2026) also cites a Gartner survey of CFOs showing AI spending rising while headcount and pay growth slow (figures not extracted). — [Journal of Accountancy, Feb 20, 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/feb/can-ai-actually-lead-to-a-heavier-workload/)
- Microsoft WTI 2026: 66% of AI users spend more time on high-value work; 58% produce work they couldn't a year ago; only 13% rewarded for reinvention when results fall short; 65% fear falling behind. — [Microsoft WorkLab, May 5, 2026](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization)
- Census HTOPS (March 2026 wave): among weekly AI users, 56% saved ≤2 hours; 30% saved 3+ hours; 14% saved nothing or lost time. The survey does not ask how time is used. — [Census Bureau, Aug 11, 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/08/ai-use-at-work.html)
- Glean Work AI Index 2026 (vendor survey; 6,000 full-time digital workers in US/UK/Australia): three in four say AI makes them more productive, estimating ~11 hours saved per week, yet only 13% say their organization is performing significantly better as a result. — [Glean Work AI Index 2026 (vendor)](https://www.glean.com/work-ai-institute/reports/work-ai-index)
- Secondary summaries cite a Federal Reserve estimate that generative AI saves ~5.4% of work hours (~2.2 hours per 40-hour week) among users, falling to ~1.3–1.4% of total hours once non-users are included, and that among daily users 33.5% saved 4+ hours vs 11.5% of once-a-week users; the primary Fed publication and its 2026 date were not confirmed. — [AI Business Weekly (secondary, unverified)](https://aibusinessweekly.net/p/ai-productivity-statistics)
- Gallup Q1 2026: large AI-adopting organizations (10,000+) report reductions (33%) more often than expansion (30%), the reverse of non-adopters. — [Gallup, Apr 12, 2026](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/704225/rising-adoption-spurs-workforce-changes.aspx)
- Indeed Hiring Lab, Sept 17, 2026: advertised-pay premium in AI-exposed jobs is "negligible at entry level" (~2 points) vs large for senior roles — i.e., whatever wage dividend exists is not reaching juniors. — [Indeed Hiring Lab, Sept 17, 2026](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/09/17/ai-exposure-isnt-squeezing-advertised-pay-in-the-us-its-boosting-it/)

### Inferences
- The pattern across Korn Ferry (efficiency up 63% / tasks up 52%), SHRM (volume and pace up for a majority), Microsoft (13% rewarded), and Glean (11 hours saved / 13% see org improvement) is consistent with an "absorption" story: individual time savings are converted into additional tasks and faster pace rather than shorter hours, higher pay, or measurable organizational performance.
- The Gallup large-firm reduction pattern plus Toothacre's layoff/reorg explanation suggests a second channel: headcount is held flat or cut on the assumption of AI productivity, so the remaining ICs inherit the work.
- Indeed's negligible entry-level pay premium and Stanford's "adjustment in employment, not base pay" together indicate that juniors are not receiving a monetary share of AI gains; the senior-level pay premium indicates the gains that are returned to workers accrue to experienced ICs and above.

### Gaps
- No 2026 source quantified hours actually worked per week by ICs before vs after AI adoption, or documented any firm returning saved time to ICs as shorter hours (no 2026 four-day-week-via-AI evidence located).
- The Fed hours-saved estimate (5.4%) is known only via secondary aggregators; the primary publication was not identified.
- Glean is a vendor with a commercial interest in AI adoption; its 11-hour figure is self-estimated and likely overstated relative to Census.
- No 2026 data was found on whether senior ICs (staff/principal without reports) experience gains differently from juniors; all level breakouts found are leaders/directors vs managers vs ICs (SHRM, Gallup, Microsoft).

## Cross-cutting evidence-quality notes for the report writer

- Observed vs self-reported: Hiring/headcount claims (Q1) rest on observed payroll (ADP), administrative (Census), and postings (Indeed, Lightcast, Revelio) data from multiple institutions and are the most robust findings in this file. Workload, expectations, slop, and burnout claims (Q2, Q3, Q6) rest entirely on self-reported surveys; Microsoft's telemetry covers Copilot usage patterns, not hours.
- Sample sizes: Gallup n=23,717 (Q1 2026); Microsoft n=20,000 AI users (10 countries, narrowed from 31,000/31 countries); Korn Ferry n=16,000+; SHRM n=5,875; Glean n=6,000; ResumeTemplates n=1,250 (no methodology reported); Census CES paper ~6.7 million graduate records; Dallas Fed ADP ~25 million workers; HBR/KPMG n=523; Anthropic n=52.
- Causality: Stanford, Dallas Fed, Indeed, Census, NY Fed, and the Fed Board all explicitly disclaim causal attribution to AI. Only the WEF (Mar 2026) framing is strongly causal.
- Contradictions to carry forward: (1) Stanford/Dallas Fed/Census vs NY Fed/Fed Board on whether entry-level effects are AI-driven; (2) occupation-level entry declines (Stanford, Indeed, Revelio June 2026) vs firm-level growth at heavy AI adopters (Ramp–Revelio June 30, 2026); (3) Indeed's internal −6.2% vs −6.3% entry-level figures; (4) secondary sources' 13% vs primary 15% for Stanford's prior-vintage gap.
- Dates not confirmed as 2026: UC Berkeley/HBR intensification study (reported Feb 2026, unverified); Anthropic 52-engineer study (Jan or Feb 2026); PwC 2026 AI Jobs Barometer (not located); Upwork figures (likely 2024–2025); Fed 5.4% hours estimate (not located).
