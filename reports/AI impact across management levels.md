# AI Hits Juniors and Managers Hardest

No: the 2026 evidence (publications dated January–October 2026) shows AI's effects are concentrated by level, not spread proportionally, and the concentration runs in opposite directions at the two ends of the pyramid. Entry-level workers are absorbing the labor-market cost: in ADP payroll data, employment of **22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations sits 19% below trend while experienced workers show no gap**, and U.S. senior job postings rose 13.5% since January 2025 as entry-level postings fell 6.3%. Managers, above all first-line "player-coaches" and the middle layer, are absorbing the workload shock: **42% of organizations cut management roles in the past year, 55% of remaining managers say they are exhausted, global manager engagement fell to 22%**, and roughly half of directors and senior managers report spending more time reviewing their teams' AI-assisted output. Executives report the largest gains (over 40% of C-level respondents say AI saves them 8+ hours a week; 79% of CEOs versus 51% of individual contributors report efficiency gains) yet barely touch the tools themselves (nearly 70% use AI under an hour a week), and their added load is governance, board scrutiny and accountability for unproven returns rather than hands-on work. Information volume is the least-measured dimension of all: the only observed 2026 data are software-engineering telemetry showing per-reviewer load roughly doubled with senior engineers absorbing it, and workplace telemetry showing email and chat time doubled after AI adoption with no level split. Nearly every workload figure is self-reported, a large share comes from vendors with a commercial stake, and the attribution of the entry-level slump to AI is actively disputed by the New York Fed and the OECD.

## Reported gains climb the org chart while measured use barely does

Every 2026 study that splits AI adoption by level finds the same monotonic gradient, and it has widened rather than closed. Gallup's Q2 2026 U.S. panel puts frequent use (a few times a week or more) at **51% for leaders, 36% for managers and 26% for individual contributors, up from 17%, 15% and 9% in 2023** ([Gallup](https://gallup.com/workplace/713063/ai-workplace-productivity.aspx)). Gallup's April wave, conditioned on the organization actually providing tools, narrows but does not erase the gap at 67% / 52% / 46% for leaders, managers and ICs, implying that part of the IC shortfall is provisioning rather than preference ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/704252/workplace-separates-adopters-holdouts.aspx)). Section's June 2026 survey of 5,026 U.S. knowledge workers finds C-suite respondents more than twice as likely as ICs to have access to agent-capable tools, and **82% of the C-suite excited about AI against 30% of ICs** ([Section](https://www.sectionai.com/ai/ai-proficiency-report-2026)). The one 2025 reference point with a four-tier split, Slack's Workforce Index, showed the same shape (daily use 43% executives, 35% senior managers, 23% middle managers, 10% ICs), and no 2026 edition appeared ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/daily-ai-workforce-use-growth/)).

Self-reported benefit follows the same slope and is steeper. Section's January 2026 edition, reported via the Wall Street Journal and Computerworld, found **more than 40% of C-level executives saying AI saves them over eight hours a week while about two-thirds of non-management staff said it saves them under two hours or nothing**, with many non-managers describing AI as "stressful and difficult to use correctly" because output must be checked and redone ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4121595/managers-and-employees-disagree-on-how-much-ai-improves-work-efficiency.html)); 40% of non-management workers said it saved no time at all ([Sherwood News](https://www.sherwood.news/tech/survey-ceos-and-workers-have-wildly-different-thoughts-on-ai-productivity/)). Korn Ferry's Workforce 2026 survey of more than 16,000 professionals from IC to CEO reports **79% of CEOs but only 51% of ICs seeing improved efficiency from AI** ([Korn Ferry](https://www.kornferry.com/about-us/press/korn-ferry-workforce-2026-report-unlocking-growth-requires-rethinking-how-work-gets-done)). SHRM's survey of 5,875 U.S. workers finds the expectation shock also tilted upward: **70% of directors and above, 55% of managers and 35% of ICs say AI has raised performance expectations** ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)). At least nine 2026 datasets quantify a leader–employee perception gap, and all point the same way: 88% of executives versus 21% of employees think employees have adequate tools and 61% versus 9% trust AI for business-critical decisions (WalkMe, a vendor) ([WalkMe](https://www.walkme.com/news-releases/enterprises-lose-51-workdays-per-employee-to-technology-friction-annually-despite-record-ai-investment-walkme-global-study-of-3750-finds)); 70% of executives call AI gains "significant" against 25% of sub-managerial workers (Lucidworks, a vendor, sample size undisclosed) ([Lucidworks via American Press](https://smb.americanpress.com/article/Lucidworks-2026-AI-Benchmark-Study-Reveals-Glaring-Disconnects-Around-AI-Benefits/6a312fe245b635ab6be4e6dd)); 76% of executives believe employees are excited about AI while 31% of employees are ([People Element via SBAM](https://www.sbam.org/the-ai-enthusiasm-gap/)).

The crucial caveat is that the executives reporting the largest savings are the people using AI least. The Stanford/NBER survey of more than 6,000 CEOs, CFOs and senior executives finds **nearly 70% use AI at work less than one hour a week and 28% never do; U.S. executives average 1.7 hours a week against 1.8 for their employees**, prompting Nicholas Bloom to say "employees know best. Their bosses have drunk the Kool Aid" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/13/ceos-ai-mandate-employees-jobs-survey-nicholas-bloom)). A General Assembly/EZRA survey of 500+ leaders shows their use is dominated by information search (69%), summarizing (68%) and drafting email (58%), with scenario planning and organizational design under 30% ([AccessNewswire](https://www.accessnewswire.com/newsroom/en/business-and-professional-services/business-leaders-still-primarily-use-ai-for-simple-tasks-study-f-1167022)). Executive perception also exceeds measured outcomes: an Atlanta/Richmond Fed working paper on roughly 750 executives describes a "productivity paradox" where AI-attributed gains exceed measured ones ([Atlanta Fed](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/publications/working-papers/2026/03/25/04-artificial-intelligence-productivity-and-the-workforce-evidence-from-corporate-executives)), and an NBER executive survey finds **more than 90% of firms report no measurable employment effect and 89% no measurable productivity effect** from AI over three years ([People Matters](https://www.peoplematters.in/news/ai-and-emerging-tech/90percent-of-firms-see-no-measurable-ai-impact-on-productivity-or-jobs-study-51625)). The honest reading is that the "executives gain most" finding is a perception and access gradient, not a measured productivity gradient: the eight hours executives report saving are plausibly hours saved by people below them producing faster, not hours the executive spent in a chat window. Vendors (Section, WalkMe, Lucidworks, BambooHR, WRITER, Workday) dominate this literature; the independent corroboration comes from Gallup, Korn Ferry, SHRM and the Fed/NBER papers.

## Entry-level workers pay through hiring, not layoffs or pay

The best-evidenced level effect in 2026 is also the one that does not show up in anyone's workload survey, because the people affected are not employed. Stanford's revised "Canaries in the Coal Mine" (August 12, 2026), using ADP payroll data through June 2026, finds **workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations roughly 19% below the employment level they would have reached had they tracked less-exposed peers, up from 15% in the July 2025 vintage, while "experienced workers show no comparable gap"**; employment of this age group in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11% from November 2022 while the same ages in the least exposed quintiles grew about 10% ([Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)). The adjustment runs through reduced hiring rather than separations and through employment rather than base pay; the authors call the results descriptive, not causal, and note the gap shrinks when education is controlled for and that some trends predate generative AI ([Stanford](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/)). The Dallas Fed, also using ADP data on roughly 25 million workers, reaches the same mechanism: the young-worker decline in exposed occupations "is primarily driven by a decline in people transitioning directly from out of the workforce into employment rather than by layoffs" ([Dallas Fed](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0106)). A Census Bureau working paper covering about 6.7 million graduate records finds that after late 2022, graduates in the most AI-exposed decile of majors saw their **probability of initial employment fall 5 percentage points and initial earnings fall about 13%**, "comparable in magnitude to the earnings losses associated with graduating into a large recession" ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html)).

Postings and firm-level data show the same seniority tilt. Indeed Hiring Lab reports **senior postings up 14.7% year-over-year as of May 2026 and entry-level postings down 7.5%; relative to January 2025, senior +13.5%, mid-level −6.7%, entry −6.3%**, with software development the extreme case at 69.3% senior and 4.5% entry-level postings ([Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/the-labor-market-is-tilting-toward-seniority/)). A Stanford/King's College study of 1.25 billion postings and 154 million employment records across 41 countries, reported by Bloomberg, finds that at AI-adopting firms **senior headcount rose about 6.7% over five years while junior headcount fell about 3%** ([The Star/Bloomberg](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/09/22/ai-adoption-is-driving-hiring-but-mostly-for-senior-roles)); the paper itself was not accessed. Revelio Labs finds a 10-point rise in AI exposure associated with an 11% drop in entry-level demand after controlling for industry and time ([Revelio Labs](https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/macro/is-ai-responsible-for-the-rise-in-entry-level-unemployment)), while its own Ramp-linked study finds firms investing heavily in AI grew employment about 10%, entry-level included, a firm-level counterpoint that reconciles with the occupation-level story only if adopters grow overall while tilting their mix senior ([Revelio Labs](https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/ai-and-work/greater-ai-investment-more-hiring)). Whatever wage dividend exists bypasses juniors: Indeed finds the advertised-pay premium in AI-exposed jobs "large for senior roles, moderate at mid-level, and negligible at entry level" ([Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/09/17/ai-exposure-isnt-squeezing-advertised-pay-in-the-us-its-boosting-it/)).

The dissent is serious and should not be buried. The New York Fed, using Lightcast postings and an Anthropic exposure measure, finds that within highly exposed occupations **junior and senior postings move "broadly in parallel" after 2022, that the exposed-versus-unexposed divergence began before 2022, and that AI "is not the main driver of the slowdown in hiring"** ([NY Fed Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/)). A second NY Fed post attributes about 64% of the rise in young-graduate unemployment to remote work and shows the age gap persists holding AI exposure constant ([NY Fed](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/06/remote-work-leaves-younger-workers-sidelined/)); a Federal Reserve Board note finds "no evidence of a reduction in job postings" at firms with higher AI adoption ([Federal Reserve Board](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/ai-adoption-and-firms-job-posting-behavior-20260327.html)); and the OECD says current evidence does not attribute the weaker youth labor market to AI ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/blogs/2026/08/young-people-labour-markets-and-ai-what-oecd-evidence-shows.html)). Indeed itself declines to blame AI, citing remote work, rate hikes and post-pandemic correction. The split is partly a measurement artifact: postings measure advertised demand, payroll data measure realized hires, and firms can post junior roles, fill fewer of them or fill them with experienced candidates. The shape of the outcome (juniors down, seniors up) is agreed across camps; the cause is not.

For the juniors who are hired, the 2026 literature describes a learning trap rather than a measured workload change. MIT Technology Review's Georgios Petropoulos argues that entry-level drafting, triage, coding and summarizing "functions as part of the economy's training system," so if AI absorbs them "firms may gain short-run efficiency while society loses long-run capability" ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/26/1137865/its-time-to-address-the-looming-crisis-in-entry-level-work/)). An HBR-published field study of 523 early-career KPMG staff frames AI as "resetting the baseline" for acceptable output and finds that foundational skills did not predict which juniors worked well with AI ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/07/research-why-some-junior-employees-work-well-with-ai-and-others-dont)); the full archetype results were not retrievable. The one direct learning measurement, an Anthropic experiment with 52 mostly junior engineers in which the AI-assisted group scored 50% versus 67% on a knowledge test, is known only through secondary coverage and its exact date and primary text were not confirmed ([Allwork.Space](https://allwork.space/2026/05/ai-apprenticeship-is-the-new-career-ladder-for-a-workforce-losing-its-training-ground/)). Juniors' information burden looks different from everyone else's: in Omni Calculator's survey of 705 AI-using workers, entry-level ICs are heavier self-checkers than experienced ICs (42% versus 30% run most of their writing through AI), 32% of all respondents feel less confident in their own writing since using AI (42% of Gen Z), and "reassurance-driven" reliance peaks among entry-level ICs and executives at 21% each ([Omni Calculator](https://www.omnicalculator.com/reports/ai-second-opinion-survey)). That is a small vendor-run sample, but it is the only 2026 source that separates junior from experienced ICs on any information measure.

## Individual contributors convert saved hours into more tasks, and senior engineers inherit the review queue

For employed ICs, every 2026 survey located reports a net workload increase alongside AI adoption, and none reports anyone working fewer hours. Korn Ferry's 16,000-person sample has **63% saying AI improved their efficiency while 52% say AI tools increased the number of tasks expected of them; 62% say workloads grew significantly over two years, 61% perform the responsibilities of more than one role, 45% are "too busy to deliver meaningful results," and 64% say their company prioritizes efficiency gains over new ways to add value** ([Journal of Accountancy](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/sep/driving-efficiency-or-driving-workers-toward-burnout-how-ai-is-being-used/)). SHRM finds more than three in five workers reporting higher work volume and 54% a faster pace since AI tools arrived ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/navigating-ai-in-the-workplace/full-report)). A ResumeTemplates survey of 1,250 corporate workers (no methodology disclosed) has 31% reporting a heavier workload against 16% lighter ([Journal of Accountancy](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/feb/can-ai-actually-lead-to-a-heavier-workload/)). The actual time saved is modest: the Census Bureau's March 2026 Household Pulse wave finds that among weekly AI users **56% saved two hours or less and 14% saved nothing or lost time** ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/08/ai-use-at-work.html)), and Workday (a vendor) reports nearly 40% of gross AI time savings lost to rework, with employees spending up to two hours a week correcting output ([Workday](https://newsroom.workday.com/2026-01-14-New-Workday-Research-Companies-Are-Leaving-AI-Gains-on-the-Table)). Workday's is also the one dataset said to show gross savings "broadly consistent across individual contributors and managers," the main 2026 evidence for proportionality on workload, and it rests on a single trade-press summary ([No Jitter](https://www.nojitter.com/ai-automation/workday-report-finds-that-ai-helps-workers-reclaim-time)).

The only observational (non-survey) study of knowledge-worker workload, an eight-month HBR-published field study of roughly 200 employees at one U.S. tech company, concludes that AI "doesn't reduce work, it intensifies it," through task expansion (designers and product managers doing engineering work they previously avoided, which added coaching and review work for engineers), blurred boundaries and constant multitasking ([Sherwood News](https://www.sherwood.news/tech/new-study-finds-ai-doesnt-reduce-work-it-intensifies-it/)). Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index, a survey of 20,000 AI-using knowledge workers, captures the incentive asymmetry: **65% fear falling behind if they do not adapt quickly, but only 13% say they are rewarded for AI-driven reinvention when results fall short**, and leaders are twice as likely as employees (21% versus 10%) to say reinvention is rewarded regardless of outcome ([Microsoft WorkLab](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization)). Gallup finds 21% of leaders but 13% of ICs rating AI's productivity impact "extremely positive" ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/704225/rising-adoption-spurs-workforce-changes.aspx)). The IC-level pattern is an absorption story: individual time savings are converted into more tasks and faster pace, not shorter hours or higher pay, while headcount is held flat or cut on the assumption of AI productivity.

Senior staff without direct reports are the least separately measured tier in the whole 2026 literature; Gallup, Korn Ferry, SHRM and Microsoft all fold them into "individual contributors." The exception is software engineering, where telemetry is unusually rich and points one way. Faros AI's data on roughly 22,000 developers finds that in high-AI-adoption teams **median pull-request review time rose 441.5%, PR size 51.3% and bugs per PR 54%, with senior engineers "disproportionately absorbing this load"** ([Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-code-quality-senior-engineer-review-burden)). A Harvard working paper on Jellyfish data from 718 firms reports code volume up 30% and pull requests up 23% per month with **no statistically significant increase in resolved issues**, review time up 49% and senior engineers absorbing most of the added burden; this is known via an AI-authored article and the paper was not fetched ([Forkast](https://forkast.news/ai-coding-agents-write-30-more-code-and-resolve-zero-additional-issues-harvard-study-finds/)). A longitudinal study of 196,212 PRs at one enterprise under a "2x" mandate finds per-capita throughput reached 2.09x baseline with gains "broadly shared across seniority levels," while **per-reviewer load roughly doubled and automated review overtook human review** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01904)). LinearB's benchmark of 8.1 million PRs shows AI-assisted PRs waiting about 17.6 hours for reviewer pickup against roughly 200 minutes for unassisted ones ([LinearB](https://linearb.io/blog/8-million-prs-engineering-productivity)). In the one domain where information volume is actually counted, then, the volume shock lands on whoever holds review authority, which in engineering is senior ICs and first-line technical leads, not on the people generating the output. Outside engineering, experienced ICs may be the calmest tier: in Omni Calculator's data they have the lowest self-verification rate (30%) and lowest reassurance-driven reliance (12%) of any role ([Omni Calculator](https://www.omnicalculator.com/reports/ai-second-opinion-survey)), but that is one small vendor survey, and no 2026 source measures senior ICs' hours or message load.

## Managers absorb both the delayering and the verification bill

The middle of the pyramid is squeezed from two directions in 2026, by headcount removal and by a new review burden, and the two reinforce each other. Korn Ferry finds **42% of organizations cut management roles in the past year, 55% of the managers who remain say they are exhausted, and 39% of workers say the lack of managers leaves them directionless** ([Korn Ferry](https://www.kornferry.com/about-us/press/korn-ferry-workforce-2026-report-unlocking-growth-requires-rethinking-how-work-gets-done)). Gallup's span-of-control study reports the **average U.S. manager's direct reports rose from 10.9 in 2024 to 12.1 in 2025**, though the median held at five to six and the average was pulled up by a two-point rise in teams of 25 or more, so flattening is concentrated, not universal ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/700718/span-control-optimal-team-size-managers.aspx)). Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 shows **global manager engagement falling from 31% in 2022 to 27% in 2024 to 22% in 2025, the largest single-year drop on record, while IC engagement barely moved (20% to 19%)**; leaders report more daily stress (46%), anger, sadness and loneliness than ICs ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx)). The company announcements match: Amazon's 16,000 January cuts were framed as "reducing layers," Meta's roughly 8,000 May cuts "disproportionately affected managers," Uber cut managers by 20% and one- or two-person "micro-teams" by about half in September, and Cloudflare's CEO said "the vast majority of those we laid off last week were measurers" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/25/the-running-list-major-tech-layoffs-in-2026-where-employers-cited-ai/); [Transport Topics/Bloomberg](https://www.ttnews.com/articles/uber-cut-3300-jobs-management)). Notably, neither Amazon's nor Uber's CEO attributed the cuts to AI; the stated rationale was bureaucracy and fragmented ownership, with AI more often the budget justification than the mechanism. Challenger, Gray & Christmas counts AI as the leading cited reason for 120,136 announced U.S. cuts year-to-date through September (about 21%), but publishes no breakdown by management level ([Challenger](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Challenger-Report-September-2026.pdf)). The first reversal signal appeared in September, when Meta began asking ICs in its Applied AI division whether they want to return to manager roles ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/12/meta-year-of-efficiency-managers-ai-investment/)); it is one division at one company. Gartner's much-repeated 2024 forecast that 20% of organizations would use AI to eliminate more than half of middle-management positions by 2026 has not been validated by any 2026 measurement found.

On top of thinner ranks, managers have become the first accountable reviewers of AI-assisted output. monday.com's Nielsen-fielded survey of 1,701 directors and senior managers finds **47% spend more time reviewing and guiding team output (59% in Australia, where a third report longer working days), 53% say AI has made it harder to evaluate original contributions, 78% have changed how they evaluate team members, 58% expect to oversee more AI agents than human employees within 12 months, 46% worry about losing control of agents and 35% fear being held accountable for agent decisions** ([monday.com](https://monday.com/blog/project-management/monday-ai-report-2026/)); TechCrunch's headline on the report was that for managers AI is "mostly a headache for now" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/21/ai-promises-productivity-but-for-managers-its-mostly-a-headache-for-now-says-new-survey/)). This is a vendor survey, but it is corroborated independently. Founder Reports' Prolific panel of 2,078 U.S. workers finds **57% of managers have had to fix or redo colleagues' over-AI-reliant work against 38% of ICs, rising to 63% of VPs and C-suite**, with 77% of all respondents reviewing AI-assisted work more carefully than human work ([Founder Reports](https://founderreports.com/ai-is-adding-to-your-managers-workload/)). HBR's interview study at two consulting firms finds senior leaders expanding scope and juniors reporting large productivity gains while middle managers were "overloaded with new responsibilities — validating AI outputs, identifying errors, coaching their teams in AI skills — all while facing unchanged or even increased delivery pressure," concluding "the middle layer is failing under this load" ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/06/ai-adoption-is-overloading-your-middle-managers)). Gartner's survey of 2,947 employees and managers finds 47% of managers say more is expected of them than a year ago and that managers spend roughly nine hours a week on employees' personal and emotional issues, though neither figure is AI-specific ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-29-gartner-hr-survey-finds-47-percent-of-managers-say-they-are-working-harder-than-1-year-ago)). McKinsey's State of AI 2026 groups mid-level managers with ICs on the losing side: **47% of mid-level managers and ICs report at least one negative effect of AI (pressure to take on more work, being overwhelmed by AI output) versus 31% of executives and senior managers** ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai)). WebMD Health Services' survey of 3,872 U.S. employees reports that the most productive AI users carry 4.5 times the burnout risk of peers and that middle managers carry the heaviest burnout burden; the vendor's primary PDF could not be checked, so the widely quoted "three times ICs" multiple is unverified ([Employee Benefit News](https://www.benefitnews.com/news/ai-burnout-is-surging-at-work)).

The two manager tiers are affected differently, though sources rarely separate them. Middle managers, meaning managers of managers, small-team bosses and "measurers," are the primary headcount target of 2026 cuts. First-line managers bear the workload shock: Gallup finds **97% of U.S. managers hold IC responsibilities, spending a median 40% of their time on individual work, and engagement falls below average and worsens with team size once IC load exceeds 40%**, a profile concentrated in small teams ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/700718/span-control-optimal-team-size-managers.aspx)). DDI's assessment data on more than 100,000 leaders finds only 15% of frontline leaders strong at facilitating change against 30% of mid-level leaders ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/ddi-data-reveals-only-8-of-executives-are-strong-at-leading-through-change-302669190.html)). Because delayering often converts managers to ICs rather than exiting them (Uber moved some managers to IC roles), the first-line tier is being partly refilled with demoted middle managers and enlarged teams, which Gallup's threshold finding predicts will depress engagement further. A theory paper posted in October formalizes the mechanism as a shift "from span of control to span of evaluation": agentic AI can take over execution and evaluation, but "demands on human judgment can rise even when reporting lines stay the same" ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7630858)). Where agents are already live, the reported experience is governance work: 79% of 400+ IT leaders have had to reverse an agent's action and 70% faced an untraceable failure, per a vendor survey ([Kore.ai](https://www.kore.ai/news/new-kore-ai-survey-72-of-enterprises-say-their-ai-agents-operate-with-unmanaged-risk-and-create-new-operational-burdens)). No 2026 source gives managers' weekly hours, meeting counts, or the number of AI-generated reports and dashboards reaching them; the closest proxies are monday.com's 57% saying more tools are in circulation and the 47%/57% review figures. The middle is the tier where AI's workload effect is best attested and its information effect least measured.

## Executives inherit accountability and scrutiny, not hours

The top of the pyramid shows a distinct pattern: little hands-on AI use, large reported benefit, and a growing load of decisions, board pressure and accountability for returns that have not yet materialized. BCG's AI Radar survey of 640 CEOs finds **72% now call themselves the main decision maker on AI, roughly double the prior year, and half believe their jobs are at stake if AI does not pay off**; "Trailblazer" CEOs (about 15%) spend more than eight hours a week on their own AI upskilling ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/press/15january2026-as-ai-investments-surge-ceos-take-lead)). Dataiku's Harris Poll of 900 CEOs at $500M+ firms (vendor-sponsored) finds 89% of U.S. CEOs saying their involvement in AI decisions has increased, CEOs personally influencing more than 40 business-critical decisions a year, and **72% of U.S. CEOs reporting board pressure for measurable AI outcomes, up from 61%** ([Dataiku](https://www.dataiku.com/en/company/news/global-ai-confessions-report-ceo-edition-2026)). IBM's study of 2,000 CEOs finds 64% comfortable making major strategic decisions on AI-generated input and 76% of organizations now with a Chief AI Officer, up from 26% ([IBM](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-05-04-ibm-study-ceos-are-reshaping-c-suite-roles-for-the-ai-era)); an HBR benchmark survey puts CAIO prevalence nearer 38%, a contradiction likely driven by sample differences. The returns being owned are thin: PwC's survey of 4,454 CEOs finds **56% report no significant financial benefit from AI to date and only 12% both cost and revenue gains**, with CEOs spending almost half their time on decisions with a horizon under a year ([People Matters on PwC](https://www.peoplematters.in/news/leadership/global-ceo-confidence-at-five-year-low-as-ai-divides-winners-and-laggards-pwc-survey-shows-at-davos-48108)); KPMG's AI Pulse of 2,145 senior leaders finds only 7% with established ROI and only 24% saying the CEO is formally accountable for AI outcomes ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/media/press-releases/2026/06/growing-adoption-signals-progress-as-cost-visibility-and-accountability-drive-ai-value.html)); Gartner finds 44% of CEOs struggle to demonstrate AI-driven financial returns ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-10-20-gartner-says-44-percent-of-ceos-struggle-to-demonstrate-ai-financial-returns-as-technology-becomes-central-to-growth)). Owning three-quarters of AI decisions while being formally accountable in a quarter of firms is an informal load without matching resources or metrics.

Whether executives' information load has gone up or down is not measured anywhere in 2026, but the indirect evidence points to more scrutiny and more verification, not AI filtering. PwC's survey of about 600 directors finds **82% rate the information linking AI outcomes, risks and performance as fair, poor or not provided**, and 71% say their board needs to strengthen AI skills ([Corporate Compliance Insights](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/boards-say-they-need-more-from-management-ai/)). BCG's CEO Insomnia Index reports boards asking questions "far more granular and informed than even six months ago" and advises CEOs not to outsource board memos to AI ([BCG](https://web-assets.bcg.com/2b/7c/2484f99045e58979f1d4dfd9571d/ceo-insomnia-index-apr-2026.pdf)). Finance chiefs verify directly: in Datarails' survey of 270 CFOs (vendor), **96% spend at least a tenth of their workday verifying or correcting AI output, averaging 26% of the day, and 16% have reported an AI error to leadership or the board before catching it**; they trust AI unreviewed for drafting email (89%) and meeting summaries (84%) but not for board-ready reports (5%) ([Datarails](https://www.datarails.com/research/2026-cfo-sentiments/)). Workiva (vendor) finds 84% of finance and audit executives at least somewhat confident in unreviewed AI output while 26% say internal audits caught AI errors that had already reached investors or the board ([Workiva](https://www.workiva.com/resources/executive-benchmark-survey-verification-gap)). Founder Reports' 63% rework rate for VPs and C-suite is the highest of any tier. So AI summaries probably do trim low-stakes reading at the top, but the high-stakes verification residue stays with executives, and the "AI chief of staff" category that promises to filter their inboxes exists in 2026 only as vendor content with no measured effect. What executives are buying instead is human help: Bloomberg reports chief-of-staff postings roughly doubled in a year as leaders "buckle under the stress and uncertainty of the AI age" ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/esg/americas-ceos-have-a-secret-weapon-the-chief-of-staff)).

The stress and turnover evidence is high but contradictory on AI's role. BCG's Insomnia Index of about 500 CEOs finds **average job stress of 66.7 on a 100-point scale with more than 70% above the clinical threshold, yet "generating bottom-line value from AI investment" ranks only ninth of eleven stressors (40.9), behind growth targets (73.8), costs and board expectations; AI urgency doubles to 42% among CEOs of $5B+ companies versus 28% overall** ([BCG](https://web-assets.bcg.com/2b/7c/2484f99045e58979f1d4dfd9571d/ceo-insomnia-index-apr-2026.pdf)). WRITER's vendor survey of 1,200 C-suite leaders at generative-AI-using firms instead finds 38% of CEOs reporting "high or crippling" stress about AI strategy and 64% fearing job loss if they fail the transition ([WRITER](https://writer.com/blog/enterprise-ai-adoption-survey-results-press-release/)); AlixPartners' 3,200-executive index finds 45% of CEOs fear losing their jobs, 72% find it increasingly hard to decide which disruptions to prioritize, and executives at AI-adoption leaders report higher anxiety ([AlixPartners](https://www.alixpartners.com/newsroom/2026-alixpartners-disruption-index/)). Fear is running far ahead of realized exits. Early-2026 press framed an "AI-era CEO churn machine" around Adobe's and Walmart's transitions, but that rested on record 2025 counts ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/19/ceo-ai-churn-turnover-adobe-shantanu-narayen/)), whereas Russell Reynolds' observed H1 2026 index shows **101 global CEO departures, down from 118 and the lowest first half in nine years of tracking, with departing tenure up to 9.0 years** ([Harvard Law School Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/08/13/global-ceo-turnover-index/)). No 2026 dataset tracks executive headcount; the nearest figure, Live Data Technologies' finding that public-company manager headcount fell 6.1% and executive headcount 4.6% from May 2022 to May 2025, is 2025 context and shows the top thinned too, but less ([Careerminds](https://careerminds.com/blog/the-great-flattening)). The executive layer in 2026 is being reshaped (new CAIO seats, converging talent and technology roles, record CFO churn) rather than shrunk, and its AI-related load is decision and accountability load, not tool use or hours. Nearly all of this evidence is consultancy or vendor self-report fielded between Q4 2025 and Q2 2026; the only observed series (turnover counts, postings) show stabilization at the top.

## Information volume is the measurement hole, and what exists points upward

The question of whether each level receives the same change in information volume cannot be answered from 2026 publications with level-segmented observed data, because none exists. The largest telemetry dataset, ActivTrak's analysis of 443 million hours from 163,638 employees, finds that in a 10,584-user subset tracked 180 days before and after AI adoption **time in email rose 104%, chat and messaging 145% and business-management apps 94%, with "no activity category decreased"**; focus sessions shrank to about 13 minutes, Saturday and Sunday productive hours rose 46% and 58%, and AI tools per organization rose from two to seven ([ActivTrak](https://www.activtrak.com/blog/2026-state-of-the-workplace/)). This is the strongest sign that written volume grew for adopters, but it comes from one vendor's customer base, measures time rather than message counts, and has no role or level split. Microsoft, whose June 2025 "infinite workday" telemetry (interruptions every two minutes, 52% of leaders and 48% of employees finding work chaotic) remains the baseline everyone cites, did not refresh those per-user figures in its May 2026 Work Trend Index; the 2026 edition reports Copilot prompt categories and a 15x rise in active agents, and an independent methodology review calls its headline 67%/32% finding "a strategic argument supported by survey-grade evidence, not as measurement" ([The Microsoft Cloud Blog](https://themicrosoftcloudblog.com/2026/05/2026-work-trend-index-evidence-check/)). No 2026 Slack Workforce Index, Grammarly/Harris, Asana or Zoom report with level-split volume data was found.

What the self-report data show is that the cost of low-quality AI output rises with seniority while its sources are everywhere. The 2025 BetterUp/Stanford baseline found managers (54%) more likely than ICs (38.5%) to receive "workslop," but with about 40% of it flowing laterally, 18% upward and 16% downward ([BetterUp](https://www.betterup.com/blog/hidden-costs-workslop)); no 2026 replication exists. The 2026 updates split: Founder Reports' 38% / 57% / 63% rework gradient across ICs, managers and VPs implies net upward flow of the burden, while Zety's survey of 1,000 workers finds **55% have received workslop from a supervisor**, implying heavy downward flow too, though Zety's fieldwork date is inconsistent across sources ([Allwork.Space](https://allwork.space/2026/03/55-of-employees-receive-ai-workslop-from-managers-or-supervisors/)). AI-generated downward communication is documented: 39% of directors and senior managers use AI to draft performance reviews ([monday.com](https://monday.com/blog/project-management/monday-ai-report-2026/)). BCG's AI at Work survey of about 12,000 frontline employees, managers and leaders in 14 markets finds **cognitive load up at every level (41% overall) but highest among leaders (48%), 41% saying AI increased time spent on decisions and 60% saying the bar for "good enough" is higher** ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/ai-at-work-why-strategy-matters-more-than-tools)); this is the best 2026 evidence that the information effect is shared across tiers, and simultaneously that it tilts upward. The HBR/BCG "AI brain fry" study of roughly 1,500 U.S. employees ties fatigue to oversight of multiple AI systems and to expectations of more output, and found managers who made time for AI questions reduced their teams' fatigue; it collected seniority but published results by function only ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/03/09/harvard-business-review-ai-workplace-fatigue-report/)). Atlassian's State of Teams estimates a $161 billion annual "fragmentation tax" on the Fortune 500 as AI output moves through disconnected systems and finds 87% of knowledge workers lack time to coordinate when everyone is in execution mode, with executives 84% more likely to invest in tools than people ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/state-of-teams-2026)). MIT's Christian Catalini, writing in HBR, frames the structural point: "when execution is cheap due to AI, verification" becomes the scarce task, and firms must become "verification factories" ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/10/the-ai-risk-your-company-is-overlooking)).

The table below summarizes what the 2026 evidence supports for each tier the question names, and how much weight it can bear.

## Conclusion

The pyramid is not being compressed evenly; it is being reshaped into something closer to a diamond with a hollowing base and a strained waist. The level that gains the most in 2026 is the one whose members use AI least and whose reported benefits are least corroborated by measured outcomes; the level that loses the most is the one not yet inside the organization, so its loss never appears in any workload survey; and the levels that do the organization's verification, first-line managers and senior individual contributors, are where the volume of AI-generated material is landing without any 2026 dataset counting it. The "proportional" reading survives only in narrow forms: the New York Fed's point that junior and senior postings move together is an argument about what caused the entry-level decline, not about whether outcomes are tilted, and Workday's "broadly consistent" hours-saved finding describes gross savings before 40% of them are lost to rework. The two camps on the entry-level question are reconcilable rather than contradictory: AI-adopting firms are growing while shifting their mix senior, which is why payroll data show juniors losing and firm-level data show adopters hiring.

The practical implication is that any organization tracking AI's effect with a single workforce-wide metric is averaging across tiers that are moving in opposite directions. The missing measurements are specific and feasible: level-segmented telemetry on messages, review queues and after-hours work (Microsoft collected it in 2025 and did not publish it in 2026), a seniority split in Challenger's AI-attributed layoff counts, and a replication of the workslop flow study that distinguishes first-line from middle managers. Until those appear, the defensible position is that AI's workload effect is concentrated on managers and its labor-market effect on juniors, that its information effect flows toward whoever holds review authority, and that the executive tier's self-reported windfall should be read as a statement about what their organizations produce for them, not about their own hours.
